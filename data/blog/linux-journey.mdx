I've been an avid Linux user for about 15 years now, and in that time I've watched distributions rise, fall, disappear, and sometimes come back stronger than ever. It's been quite a ride.

## Where It All Started

My story starts the week I bought my very first computer. That same week, I stumbled upon Linux — and that same week, I was already trying to put it on a flash drive. The tool I used was **UNetbootin** (short for *Universal Netboot Installer*), a cross-platform utility that can create live USB systems and load a variety of Linux distributions without a CD. There was also Pen Drive Linux kicking around back then.

Using one of those tools, I managed to get Ubuntu onto a flash drive. The moment it booted — bypassing all the software configured on my machine and launching a full operating system straight from that little drive — was a genuine light-bulb moment.💡 It was the moment I truly understood how software interacted with hardware. That was **Ubuntu 9**, a long time ago.

## The Distro-Hopping Years

From there, I distro-hopped relentlessly:

- **Ubuntu** — where it all began
- **Fedora** — polished and reliable
- **OpenSUSE** — solid but niche
- **Solus** — a lesser-known gem worth trying

But I always came back to **Arch Linux**, which remains my favorite distribution to this day. There's something about building your system from the ground up that never gets old.

## Current Setup: Niri on Arch

Today I'm running **Niri** as my Wayland compositor. Niri is a scrollable-tiling Wayland compositor where windows are arranged in columns on an infinite strip going to the right — opening a new window never causes existing windows to resize, and every monitor maintains its own separate window strip. It's a genuinely fresh take on window management.

> Niri is available on Arch via `pacman` and has an active community on Matrix. Check out the [Arch Wiki page for Niri](https://wiki.archlinux.org/title/Niri) to get started.

## The Art of Linux Ricing

Speaking of customization — Linux **ricing** has absolutely taken off.

In the Linux world, "ricing" refers to the process of customizing your machine and creating a bespoke environment. The term originates from old internet slang meaning "to make it look nice," and the culture began soon after minimalist window managers like **i3** and **bspwm** started to emerge in the early Linux days.

Today, ricing touches everything:

- **GRUB** — your boot menu
- **Plymouth** — boot animations
- **Display manager** — login screen (SDDM, LightDM, etc.)
- **Shell / bar** — the workspace you live in every day

The volume of creative, open-source work the community has produced — all free — is finally getting the recognition it deserves. Communities like [r/unixporn](https://reddit.com/r/unixporn) and countless GitHub dotfile repos are proof of that.

## Why Tiling Compositors Change Everything

The big distinction worth understanding — especially if you're new to Linux — is that tools like **Niri** or **Hyprland** are designed to help you eventually scale *past* the point of needing a mouse.

Niri is highly keyboard-driven and stands out from traditional tiling window managers like i3 or Sway due to its unique scrollable-tiling model, which can significantly boost productivity and focus.

The goal is a workflow built entirely around keybindings — launching apps, switching windows, and managing your workspace without ever reaching for your mouse. You become the person clicking madly, hands flying across the keyboard like a focused machine, with a keybind for everything.

Compare that to a full desktop environment like **KDE Plasma**, which prioritizes accessibility and visual familiarity. Neither approach is wrong — it comes down to what you're optimizing for.

## A Note for New Linux Users

If you're just getting started, don't feel overwhelmed. Start with something approachable — Ubuntu, Fedora, or Linux Mint — and let curiosity take you further. The distro-hopping, the ricing, the tiling compositors — all of that is waiting for you when you're ready.

The Linux ecosystem is one of the most remarkable collaborative projects in the history of computing. Dive in.
